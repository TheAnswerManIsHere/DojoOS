## 1. One repository, one application, two processes

pnpm workspace, TypeScript, Node 20+. Three packages: `web` (Express API + React SPA via Vite, Drizzle on Postgres), `worker` (media jobs: ffmpeg/ffprobe, transcription client; no HTTP surface), `shared` (Drizzle schema, job contracts, the resolved-EDL and template types, the storage client). `web` and `worker` import `shared`; nothing is shared by copy.

Modules inside `web` are directories with a public `index.ts`, and a lint rule fails any import that reaches past another module's index: `auth`, `ingest`, `library`, `cuts`, `templates`, `render`, `feedback`. No table, route or type anticipates the LMS, CMS, CRM, payments or publishing beyond the stable identifiers every row carries.

## 2. Where it runs

**One Reserved VM deployment, one Postgres, two processes under one supervisor.** The supervisor starts `web` and `worker`, and exits non-zero if either dies so the platform restarts the deployment (measured on the scaffold, above). The job queue is the boundary between them, so the worker can move to its own deployment later without any caller changing; that split happens when render load or reliability demands it, not before.

Reasoning: a Repl carries one deployment (measured 2026-09-29) and a second Repl would need an externally reachable database, which Replit documents for production databases but which adds a second billing line and a second thing to break for a single operator. One VM at 2 vCPU / 8 GB ($50/month) carries the 30-hour backlog as an overnight batch and 1 to 5 hours a month after that; the size moves if the first 4K render says so.

**Working space.** Each job gets a bounded temp directory under the deployment's disk, cleared on completion or failure, and the working set is bounded by construction, not by the size of the largest file:

- *Ingest* stages one original, produces the proxy, sprite and peaks from it, uploads them, and deletes the original. Peak: one original plus its derivatives (under 45 GB plus 3 GB at the 4K worst case; about 6 GB plus 3 GB for today's files).
- *Render* never stages a cut's sources together. For each source the cut uses, in order: stage the original, extract every span the resolved EDL takes from it into an intermediate clip (same codec, no re-encode where the span boundaries allow, otherwise a mezzanine encode), delete the original. Then build the filtergraph over the intermediates and write the output. Peak: the largest single original plus intermediates totalling the cut's own duration plus the output. A 60-minute lesson at the 4K worst case is one 45 GB original plus about 45 GB of intermediates plus a 10 GB output, about 100 GB; at today's sizes about 20 GB. **That figure is an estimate, not a demonstrated bound**: the intermediates' size depends on the encoding chosen for them, and transition overlaps and independently retained narration mean the intermediate material need not equal the cut's duration. Scaffold step 0 budgets the real intermediate encoding and spans beside the output when it measures the disk.
- The deployment's writable disk is a **named unknown**: the dev workspace reports 254 GB free (`df -h .`, Replit Agent, 2026-09-29), and Replit documents a published deployment as a separate instance whose filesystem is not to be relied on for persistence. Scaffold step 0 measures it and records the number in the repository's architecture page. If it is under the render peak above, ffmpeg reads originals over HTTPS range requests from signed URLs instead of staging them (the scaffold's one test of that path was inconclusive, so it is verified against B2 objects at that point), and the intermediate-plus-output bound still holds.

**Recoverability.** A job is a row: `queued → claimed → running → done | failed`, with `attempts` and a `claimed_at` lease. A worker restart leaves `running` rows whose lease has expired; the claim query reclaims them. A crash *before* the lease expires leaves a row the startup poll cannot yet take, so whenever the worker knows of unfinished work — any row it holds, or any `running` row it saw at startup — it schedules one claim attempt for that row's lease expiry; the timer exists only while work is in flight, so the idle cost in §10 is unchanged, and no job waits for the six-hour safety poll to be recovered. Every job writes its outputs to deterministic storage keys derived from its inputs, so a re-run overwrites rather than duplicates, and marks the row `done` only after the last upload completes. Nothing about a job lives only in the temp directory.

**What is verified and what is not.** Verified on the scaffold (Replit Agent, 2026-09-29, quoted): the supervisor exits non-zero when a child dies (`worker exited unexpectedly (1); stopping the deployment … SUPERVISOR_EXIT=1`); ffmpeg and ffprobe 6.1.2 on PATH. Not yet verified, and verified at scaffold step 0 before any feature is built on it: that the published deployment restarts after the supervisor exits; that a `running` job killed before its lease expires is reclaimed and completes after that restart with no further upload and no manual nudge; the deployment's writable disk. Any of the three failing reopens the hosting choice before dependent features accumulate.

**Throughput is an estimate until step 1 measures it.** A 1080p proxy encode on 2 vCPU is assumed at one to two times realtime, so the 30-hour library takes 15 to 30 hours of worker time; 4K originals are slower by their pixel count. Probe time says nothing about this. Step 1 records the first real proxy's wall time and peak memory, and the worker size or concurrency moves on that number.

## 3. Storage

Backblaze B2 through the S3 API, prefixes `originals/`, `proxies/`, `renders/`, one private bucket; R2 is the fallback if B2's free egress allowance (three times stored volume per month) proves tight under preview playback. The storage client in `shared` speaks S3 only.

Upload is browser-resumable and direct: the SPA asks `web` to create a multipart upload, `web` issues presigned part URLs, the browser puts parts to B2, `web` completes the upload and enqueues ingest. No file byte passes through the Repl. Playback and render inputs use short-lived signed GET URLs.

Sizing: 180 GB to 1.3 TB in year one, $1 to $10 a month on B2; the per-hour figure is measured on the first real file.

The job's traffic shape: 0.2 to 1.3 TB stored in year one; egress dominated by proxy streaming for preview (proxies are about a quarter of originals) and originals pulled at render; ingress free everywhere. ## 4. The media pipeline

- **Ingest** (worker, on upload completion): ffprobe → source metadata; the original read once to produce a 1080p H.264/AAC proxy, a thumbnail sprite and a waveform peaks file; the proxy's audio sent to a hosted transcription provider for word-timed text; source marked `ready`. Audio is the camera's lav track (Jared); no sync step. The source model carries a `role` so a second camera or a separate audio file fits later without a schema change.
- **Deck ingest** (worker, D5): a source with role `deck` is a PowerPoint file or a PDF exported from one. The job converts it to a PDF where needed (LibreOffice headless, a Nix package in the VM), rasterises one image per page at the output resolution, extracts the speaker notes and title per slide, and writes `slides` rows (index, image key, notes). A page is a *step*: a slide, or a build within a slide when the deck supplies one page per build. **In-slide animations are a named unknown**: server-side rendering yields one static image per page and nothing moves inside it. Verified on one of Jared's real decks at the deck editor's first version; if his decks rely on builds, the fallback is one slide per build state in PowerPoint (a duplicate-and-reveal convention), which arrives as ordinary pages. No animation engine is built.
- **Preview** (browser): the resolved EDL (below) is played by seeking within proxies over signed range requests; a dissolve uses two video elements; captions, the framing region and the vertical crop box are overlays; in deck mode a slide image sits under or beside a positioned video element. No render wait.
- **Render** (worker): one ffmpeg filtergraph built deterministically from a revision's resolved EDL: trims and concat, `xfade` transitions, crop/scale from the framing region for the target orientation, burned captions, template bumpers and lower thirds, `loudnorm` on the audio bus; in deck mode the slide images are still inputs with durations and the camera layer is an `overlay` at its placement region. Output H.264 MP4, 16:9 for a lesson, 9:16 for a short, written to `renders/` with its metadata (title, chapters, duration, CE minutes) and a stable identifier.
- **Transcription** is a hosted provider called from the worker behind one interface in `shared`; the provider (word timings, single speaker, cost per hour) is chosen during the build and is not this plan's decision.

**Why ffmpeg, compared rather than assumed (David, 2026-09-30: "has anyone done any research to confirm that those are the best tools for the job?").** The job is: probe files, make proxies, and render a deterministic cut (trims, concat, dissolves, crop/scale per orientation, burned captions, audio normalisation) from a machine-generated description, on a Linux server, with no human at an editor. Candidates read from their own pages on 2026-09-30:

- **ffmpeg / ffprobe** (the plan). The decoder, encoder and filter substrate that the other candidates sit on; runs anywhere; one filtergraph per render is auditable text. Cost: the filtergraph is built by our code, so complexity lives in the resolver-to-filtergraph step.
- **MLT framework** (the engine inside Shotcut and Kdenlive): "enables you to author, manage, and run multitrack audio/video compositions" from an XML project, with transition and filter plugins and a command-line renderer, `melt`. Its file reading is FFmpeg: the avformat producer is described as "Read an audio and/or video file using FFmpeg." So MLT is an EDL-shaped layer over the same substrate. It becomes the fallback if the filtergraph step grows past what one function can hold cleanly; the resolved EDL would map to MLT XML instead of a filtergraph, and nothing upstream changes.
- **GStreamer Editing Services**: "a library to simplify the creation of multimedia editing applications" with timeline, layers, clips, transitions and effects, rendering "through all the available GStreamer plugins." A different substrate with a smaller server-side track record for this shape of job; no advantage over MLT for us.
- **Hosted render APIs** (Shotstack as the representative): a JSON edit description rendered in their cloud at "$0.30 / Per minute" pay-as-you-go or "$0.20 / Per minute" on subscription, "1080p Resolution" on those plans, 4K only on the high-volume plan, with "1 credit is equal to 1 minute of video, regardless of resolution." For the 30-hour backlog rendered once as lessons that is $360 to $540, and every re-render of a corrected caption is billed again; 4K output needs the top plan. It buys us out of the render worker at the price of a vendor in the path of every export and no 4K on the plans we would be on.
- **Editly, Remotion and similar Node renderers** are wrappers over ffmpeg (Editly) or a browser plus ffmpeg (Remotion), adding a layer without removing one.

Conclusion: every credible option either is ffmpeg or wraps it, so ffmpeg is the right substrate; the only real alternative is buying the render step from a hosted API, which costs more than the machine at Jared's volumes and caps resolution. MLT is the named fallback for the filtergraph layer. This is a technical choice under the plan's authority, recorded in `decisions.md` at the sync.

## 5. The resolved EDL is the centre

Everything Jared logs and adjusts is stored as **bricks** (shared, per source) and **cut-specific overrides** (per cut, per brick). Neither is played or rendered directly. A pure function in `shared`, `resolveCut(cut, bricks, template) → ResolvedEDL`, produces the one artefact both the browser preview and the ffmpeg render consume:

```
ResolvedEDL {
  orientation: "16:9" | "9:16"
  slides:  [ { slideId, outStartMs, outEndMs, transitionOut? } ]            // the deck layer; empty for a camera-edited cut
  picture: [ { sourceId, inMs, outMs, framing: [ {atMs, region} ], placement, transitionOut? } ]
           // placement: "full" | "split" | "corner" | "hidden"  — the camera layer's region; "full" for a camera-edited cut
  narration: [ { sourceId, inMs, outMs, gain } ]        // continuous under b-roll by default
  captions: [ { startMs, endMs, text, styleRef } ]
  template: { captionStyle, defaultTransition, punchInScale, lowerThirds, bumpers, sfx, loudnessTarget }
  chapters: [ { atMs, title } ]
}
```

The preview player interprets a `ResolvedEDL`; the render builds its filtergraph from the same object. A change that reaches one and not the other is a defect by construction.

**The picture is two layers, and deck mode is the second layer being non-empty.** A camera-edited cut has an empty `slides` layer and every picture segment at `placement: full`; nothing about the v4 model changes for it. A deck cut has `slides` segments tiling the output clock (the transition times Jared chooses) and camera segments carrying one of David's three layouts: `hidden` (voice-over only, the narration still drawn from the camera source's audio), `split` (his video on the right, the deck on the left), `corner` (a small overlay upper right, the deck full). Placement is per segment, so a lesson can change layout at a slide boundary. **A slide cue is anchored to the narration, never to the output clock** (Astra, round 4): if Jared marks a page change at five minutes and then trims ten seconds of narration before it, a cue stored as an output time would drift ten seconds past the passage he meant. So the contract is: *slide advances and the layout changes tied to them retain their relationship to the selected narration when earlier material is trimmed or reordered; draft cues carry narration-relative information, and the resolver produces their output-clock times.* A `cut_slides` row therefore anchors to a narration occurrence in the cut (the `cut_bricks` row) and a source time within it; `resolveCut` maps the anchor through that brick's output placement, exactly as it maps captions and chapters, so a cue follows its words through every trim, reorder and brick edit, shared bricks included. A single slide used in a camera-edited lesson is a `slides` segment over continuing narration, which is the same mechanism as the stock illustration X below: that is "PowerPoint as b-roll", and it needs nothing further. One resolver, one preview interpreter and one filtergraph builder serve both modes, and the filtergraph's only addition is an overlay of the camera layer at its region.

**One output clock.** The resolver, not either consumer, decides editorial timing. Every picture and narration segment carries its resolved *output placement* (`outStartMs`, `outEndMs`) beside its source span (`sourceId`, `inMs`, `outMs`). A transition of duration `d` between two picture segments overlaps them by `d` on the output clock, and the narration segments underneath are placed and cross-faded over the same `d`, so picture and voice stay aligned and the cut's total length is the sum of picture spans minus the overlaps. A b-roll substitution replaces picture only, on an output-clock interval; the narration segment beneath it is unchanged. Captions, chapters and framing keyframes are expressed in output time. Proxy and original playback map to the same source times through the source's known offset, which is zero for a proxy made from the whole original. Neither preview nor render independently decides where anything falls; they differ only in what they read the pixels from.

**A revision snapshots the resolved EDL and the template values, not references.** Approving a cut writes `revisions` with the full `ResolvedEDL` JSON, the template's `spec` as it stood, the render key and a digest of the two. Later edits to a brick's out point, a template's caption style or a shared override touch only draft cuts; the approved file and its metadata cannot drift.

**Worked example.** Shoot "Upper trapezius trigger points", one camera source S. Jared logs four bricks: B1 intro (0:12–1:40), B2 demonstration (2:05–6:30), B3 the same demonstration retaken (6:50–10:55, marked alternative-of B2's beat, B3 chosen), B4 summary (11:10–12:00). A b-roll brick X is an 8-second anatomy illustration from a stock source.

- *Lesson cut* (16:9): picture [B1, B3 with X substituted over 2:20–2:28 of B3, B4], with a 1-second dissolve between B1 and B3; narration [B1, B3 whole, B4], so Jared's voice continues under X; captions off by the lesson template; chapters at B1 and B3. **On the output clock**: B1 is 88 s long, so B1 occupies 0–88 s; the dissolve overlaps 1 s, so B3 starts at 87 s and, being 245 s long, ends at 332 s; X replaces picture at 87 + 140 = 227 s to 235 s while B3's narration runs on beneath it from 87 s to 332 s; B4 follows with a hard cut at 332–382 s. Total 382 s, not 383, because the resolver subtracted the overlap once for picture and narration alike. Chapter marks: 0 s and 87 s.
- *Short cut* (9:16): picture [B3 trimmed by override to 7:10–7:55, framing keyframes keeping the hands region, no X]; narration the same span; captions from the transcript slice with two of them corrected in the caption editor; the short template's punch-in scale and caption style.

Both cuts resolve from the same four bricks and one b-roll brick; B3's trim, framing and X substitution are overrides on each cut's `cut_bricks` row, not edits to B3. Approving the lesson freezes its resolved EDL; changing B3's out point afterwards changes only the short's draft.

- *Deck lesson* (16:9, D5): shoot "Lumbar stabilisation" has one camera source S2, a single 18-minute take of Jared talking through a deck, and one deck source D of 14 pages. Jared marks one narration brick N over the whole take (trimming the start and the end), then, playing N, presses the advance-slide key at each point the next page should appear: fourteen output times. He sets layout `split` from page 1 and `corner` from page 9, where the page is a dense table. The resolver emits `slides` tiling 0 s to the end of N at his fourteen times, `picture` as N's camera segments with placement `split` then `corner`, and `narration` as N's audio; captions off; chapters from the deck's page titles. Approving it snapshots pages, placements and times together. Nothing he did used a track. *Then an earlier trim*: he moves N's in point ten seconds later, dropping a false start. Every cue is anchored to N at a source time, so page 5, marked at source 4:50 of S2, still appears as he says the words it belongs to, now ten seconds earlier on the output clock; the preview shows it and the export matches it, and that demonstration is part of the deck editor's acceptance.

**Bricks may overlap on a source, and a brick belongs only to the cuts that include it** (David, 2026-09-30: a 30-second lesson brick and a 3-second short brick will often cover the same moment of the same source). Nothing in the model gives a brick an exclusive claim on source time: `bricks` has no uniqueness on its span, so Jared can mark B3 (6:50–10:55) for the lesson and, inside it, B3s (7:12–7:15) tagged for the short, in the same watching pass; each cut includes the bricks meant for it, and its `cut_bricks` rows may trim them further. The brief's "anything not inside a brick is cut" is therefore read per cut: dead space in a cut is whatever no brick *of that cut* covers, not whatever no brick anywhere covers. "Bricks are never copied per output" (must not change) still holds: a brick that serves two cuts is shared, and a brick made for one cut is simply its own brick. The logging viewer's first-version plan owns the interaction for marking a brick inside another; the model needs nothing added. A `parent_brick` provenance link (which brick a short brick was carved from) is a later addition if the viewer wants it, not part of this plan.

**Every edit is a command, and the command set is closed.** Bricks, overrides, slide transitions and placements are never written by a screen directly. A `commands` module in `shared` holds the taxonomy as a typed, closed set (mark in, mark out, punch in with a framing hint, insert b-roll, set transition, trim, move a placement, set layout, advance slide here, and so on; the contents are the viewer's first-version plan's to fix) and one pure function, `applyCommand(cutState, command) → cutState`, which is the only path by which a cut changes. Two inputs call it:

- *Keyboard.* A keymap table, data not code, binds keys to commands and to parameter nudges: space plays and pauses, arrows move the in or out point by a frame or a second, brackets move a punch-in's placement, Esc undoes the last command. Jared's bindings change without a deploy, and the feedback rail can read which commands he actually uses.
- *Voice.* Enter opens a push-to-talk window. Speech becomes text, and a model is asked one bounded question: which command of the taxonomy is this, with what parameters, or `not understood`. The call is constrained to the taxonomy's JSON schema (structured output), the answer is validated against it before `applyCommand` runs, the applied command is shown as it lands, and Esc reverses it. "Punch in here, frame the instructor's hands in the lower third" is the punch-in command with a framing hint; the hint seeds the region and the brackets adjust it. The model never produces an edit, only a classification, which is the AI rule holding on this surface: the spoken request is Jared's decision and a misread is visible and one key from undone.

**Undo reverses an operation within the records it owns; nothing reconstructs state from a journal** (Astra, round 4). A cut depends on shared bricks that other cuts also edit, so replaying one cut's history would restore a stale brick value or erase another cut's edit. The contract: *shared bricks and cut-specific records remain authoritative; applying a command records what is needed to reverse that one operation, and undo reverses it within its owning scope, a brick's or this cut's.* Exact undo-stack mechanics are implementation choices. `cut_events` (cut, sequence, command, actor, created at) stays as a usage record only, which is what makes "which commands does he use" a query; it reconstructs nothing. **A spoken request is bound to its context the moment Enter is pressed**: the target (which brick, which slide) and the playhead are captured before speech starts, so recognition latency cannot move the edit, and the applied command carries those concrete parameters for the keys and for undo to reuse. **A framing hint names a region, it does not find one**: "the hands in the lower third" selects a named framing preset (lower third, centre, and so on from the template) that the brackets and arrows then refine. A text classifier has no way to locate hands in the picture; automatic subject location is a capability David decides, and the viewer's first-version plan asks him rather than this one assuming it. Speech-to-text (the browser's own engine, or a hosted stream) and the classifying model are chosen in the build under David's comparison rule, behind one interface each in `shared`; the taxonomy is the contract, and the model behind it can change. The browser engine is checked on Jared's actual browser before it is chosen: support is uneven and some implementations send audio to a remote service, which is a disclosure fact as much as a technical one.

## 6. Data model

All rows: ULID ids, created/updated timestamps, no hard deletes.

- `users` (email, tier ∈ {operator, tester}); `shoots`; `sources` (shoot, role ∈ {camera_a, camera_b, audio, render, deck}, nullable `origin_revision` for a source that is a rendered cut, storage keys, probe, ingest_state, duration/width/height/fps); `slides` (deck source, index, image key, title, notes); `transcripts` (source, provider, version, words); `bricks` (source, in/out, attributes, tags, beat, is_chosen); `cuts` (shoot, kind ∈ {lesson, short}, mode ∈ {camera, deck}, template, state ∈ {draft, approved}); `cut_bricks` (cut, brick, position, overrides: trim deltas, framing keyframes per orientation, transition, chosen alternative, narration_silence, placement); `cut_slides` (cut, slide, position, anchor: cut_brick and source_ms); `captions` (cut, span, text, style); `templates` (spec JSON); `revisions` (cut, number, resolved_edl, template_snapshot, render_key, metadata, digest, approved_at); `cut_events` (cut, sequence, command, actor; a usage record, never replayed); `jobs`; `feedback` and `questions` (the rail).

The scaffold created these (Replit's report); the plan changes `revisions.edl` to hold the **resolved** EDL as defined above, which is a rename plus a type, not a migration of data, and adds `sources.origin_revision` and the `render` role for the second flow below, the `deck` role with `slides` and `cut_slides` for deck mode, `placement` in overrides, `cuts.mode`, and `cut_events`.

**Two flows from shoot to short, both supported by this model (David, 2026-09-30).** *Flow A, one pass*: Jared logs the shoot once, marking lesson bricks and short bricks (overlapping, above); the short is cut from the same sources. *Flow B, two passes*: Jared logs the shoot for the lesson only, approves and renders it, and the rendered lesson is ingested as a new source (`role = render`, `origin_revision` set) that he logs again, this time for the short, watching a clean linear programme instead of raw takes. Flow B's short bricks reference the render source, and **a render-backed brick is an interval of an immutable lesson revision**, not a span of camera footage. Mapping it back to "the original source spans" is not enough, because the lesson at that interval is a *composition*: inside a dissolve two picture spans and two narration spans contribute at once; over a b-roll insert the visible picture is X while the voice is B3's; across a bumper there is generated content with no camera span at all. So the composition contract is:

> A render-backed brick identifies `[inMs, outMs)` on the output clock of an immutable lesson revision. The shared resolver clips that revision's resolved EDL to the interval — picture and narration independently, each segment's output placement and source span cut at the interval's edges, a transition kept at whatever fraction of it the interval covers — and re-bases the result to the short's own output clock. It then applies the short's framing keyframes and the short's template, and emits the `ResolvedEDL` that the short's preview and export both consume. Every clipped segment keeps its original-source coordinates, so reframing (the crop box) and the render both read camera pixels at full resolution.

That is one pure function beside `resolveCut`, `clipRevision(revision, inMs, outMs) → ResolvedEDL fragment`, and `resolveCut` calls it for a render-backed brick where it would otherwise read a camera brick's span. It is what keeps flow B from paying a generation loss or losing the 4K crop room, and it is what keeps what Jared selected — its duration, its picture, its audio — from changing between the lesson he watched and the short he gets. Exact types and filter construction are implementation choices.

## 10. Operating cost

**Design consequence first.** Replit's usage-based database bills active hours at **$0.16 per compute hour** (docs.replit.com/billing/aug-cloud-billing-updates, quoted 2026-09-30: "Compute time | Per compute hour | $0.16") and counts a database active "when they receive requests and for an additional 5-minute period after the last request" (docs.replit.com/billing/about-usage-based-billing, quoted 2026-09-29). Any timer that touches the database opens a five-minute window each time it fires: a ten-minute poll would bill about 360 hours a month, about $58, more than the machine (Astra, round 2; my v2 had proposed exactly that). So **the worker has no idle timer.** Because `web` and `worker` share one deployment, `web` nudges the worker over localhost whenever it enqueues a job, retrying the nudge and logging a failure; the worker polls the queue once at startup (which is where an interrupted job is reclaimed) and immediately after each nudge; and a safety poll every six hours catches a nudge lost to a worker restart in between, about four windows a day, about ten active hours a month, about $1.60. A missed nudge is therefore caught at the next enqueue, at the next restart, or within six hours, whichever comes first. No other component touches the database on a timer, with one bounded exception: while the worker holds or has seen unfinished work it schedules a single claim attempt at that work's lease expiry (§2, S4 round 3), which fires only when a job was interrupted and costs one window when it does.

## Runtime behaviour

Job states and lease reclaim as in §2. The worker's queue poll is nudge-driven, with a poll at startup, a claim attempt at the lease expiry of any unfinished work it knows of, and a six-hour safety poll, never a short idle timer (§2, §10). Ingest state per source is visible on the library page: uploading, uploaded, probing, proxying, transcribing, ready, failed with the error text. A failed job is retried up to three times, then left `failed` with the error shown; the operator retries from the page. Upload resumes from the last completed part after a browser reload (the multipart upload id is stored per source).

## Security, permissions, validation

Server-side session check on every route; presigned URLs scoped to one key and a short TTL; multipart part URLs valid for the upload session only; file type and size validated at upload creation; no user-supplied path reaches storage keys (keys are derived from ids). Operator actions that change data are ordinary authenticated routes; there is no admin bulk action in this increment.
